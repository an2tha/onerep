# Hypertrophy, recovery, and a useful Progress feature

Companion reviews: [calorie deficits and body recomposition](./deficit-and-recomp-evidence-review.md), and [endurance training, fueling, and recovery](./endurance-evidence-review.md).

Research date: 25 September 2026. This is a targeted evidence review, not a registered systematic review. It prioritizes peer-reviewed systematic reviews, meta-analyses, longitudinal human training studies, and relevant consensus statements. Acute mechanistic studies and weaker counterevidence are identified separately. Published abstracts and accessible full texts were used; not every included paper was available in full text. Recommendations assume a generally healthy adult pursuing muscle growth, not rehabilitation or competitive drug-assisted bodybuilding.

## What the proposed “optimal range” gets right

Muscle growth depends on a sufficient training stimulus, nutrition, and the ability to sustain training and adaptation. More work is not automatically a better decision. However, the evidence does **not** establish a universal narrow zone with a small tolerance margin that can be located from sleep, soreness, or a wearable recovery score.

Coaching terms such as minimum effective volume, maximum adaptive volume, and maximum recoverable volume can organize a discussion. They are not standardized biological thresholds with validated individual measurement procedures. They also describe different objectives: the least work that produces growth, the work that maximizes growth, and the most work someone can tolerate need not coincide.

The best recent volume analysis supports increasing average hypertrophy with additional sets, with diminishing returns. It does not identify a universal point where everyone starts losing gains. It also favors distinguishing direct from indirect muscle work. This is evidence for a dose-response relationship, not a personal prescription generated from a population curve. [Pelland et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/)

Poor sleep or inadequate energy might reduce adaptation to the same training, reduce the work someone can perform, or make a workload harder to sustain. Those are different effects. Drawing a curve that simply shifts left by a known number of sets implies a model that has not been validated.

For OneRep, the defensible goal is: **help someone find a productive, sustainable amount of training for each muscle, then adjust from observed trends.** A person's maximum tolerated workload should not become a target to chase.

## Evidence supporting, challenging, and limiting the idea

| Question | Supporting evidence | Counterevidence or limitation | Practical interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does dose matter? | The 2026 ACSM overview synthesized 137 systematic reviews and identified higher training volume as a hypertrophy lever. | Its reviews overlap and are not 137 independent replications. Many prescriptions work. [ACSM](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/) | Training consistently and doing enough challenging work matter more than finding one perfect prescription. |
| Is there an upper useful range? | A review in trained young adults suggested roughly 12–20 weekly sets as a useful range in studied muscles. [Baz-Valle et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877/) | Newer continuous modelling supports diminishing returns without a universal ceiling. High-volume data are sparse and average responses cannot identify individual limits. [Pelland et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/) | Treat set ranges as starting guidance, not diagnostic boundaries. |
| Is more volume reliably better? | The overall literature favors multiple sets and a positive average dose-response. [Schoenfeld et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/) | A methodological critique questions unusually large changes and measurement consistency in influential studies. It is a narrative critique, not an equally strong replacement meta-analysis. [Buckner et al.](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/trainology/12/2/12_29/_article) | The direction is more credible than an exact optimum or a precise prediction of extra growth per set. |
| Does sleep loss harm the anabolic environment? | Five nights with four hours in bed reduced integrated myofibrillar protein synthesis relative to eight hours. A separate randomized crossover study found 18% lower post-meal synthesis after total sleep deprivation. [Saner et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32078168/), [Lamon et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33400856/) | These are small, short mechanistic studies with severe sleep manipulation. They did not establish an equivalent percentage reduction in long-term muscle growth. | Protect sleep, but do not turn “18% lower synthesis” into “18% fewer gains.” |
| Can people grow or improve with imperfect sleep? | A small 2024 study found training improvements in both habitual short sleepers and adequate sleepers. [Borba et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39268337/) | Sleep exposure was habitual, not randomly assigned. Only 12 people were in each training group, training lasted 16 sessions, and size assessment included circumference-derived estimates. | This weakens all-or-nothing claims. It does not demonstrate that sleep is irrelevant or equivalent between conditions. |
| Does stress reduce recovery? | Higher psychological stress was associated with slower recovery of muscle function after strenuous exercise in 31 students. [Stults-Kolehmainen et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24343323/) | Observational stress exposure and an acute recovery outcome do not quantify chronic hypertrophy effects. | Ask about stress as context, not as a numerical penalty on muscle growth. |
| Does inadequate food matter? | Energy-deficit meta-analysis found impaired lean-mass gains, with strength gains relatively preserved. Its regression estimated that a deficit around 500 kcal/day offset average lean-mass gains. [Murphy and Koehler](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/) | Lean mass is not identical to skeletal muscle. The estimate is not a personal cutoff and depends on population and measurement. | Avoid prolonged substantial deficits when maximizing growth is the priority. Strength improvement alone does not prove growth. |
| Does better perceived recovery mean better growth? | Recovery can help sustain training. | Repeated post-lifting cold-water immersion may modestly attenuate hypertrophy despite its use for soreness relief. [Piñero et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11235606/) | Soreness relief and physiological adaptation are different outcomes. |

## Criteria worth acting on

Evidence labels here are judgments about the specific claim, not a formal GRADE assessment. “Stronger” means reasonably consistent longitudinal evidence; “moderate” means useful but limited or heterogeneous; “indirect” means mechanisms, associations, or coaching inference. None validates a composite OneRep score.

| Factor | What to measure | Practical starting point or response | Evidence and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly muscle-specific volume | Completed working sets, direct and indirect roles, recent baseline | Around 10 challenging sets per muscle per week is a defensible initial reference. Beginners or returners can start lower. A currently productive routine need not be changed to fit it. | Stronger for multiple sets and dose-response; uncertain exact optimum. [ACSM](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/) |
| Indirect work | Which muscles a compound exercise meaningfully trains | Show direct sets separately. If estimating fractional sets, explain assumptions and allow correction. | Counting an indirect set as 0.5 fitted one meta-analysis well; it is not a physiological constant for every exercise or person. [Pelland](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/) |
| Effort | Repetitions in reserve (RIR), meaning estimated additional repetitions possible with the same technique | Most working sets around 1–3 RIR is a practical default. Failure can be used selectively. Learn to estimate effort. | Moderate. Closer-to-failure work tends to favor growth, but exact RIR-response is uncertain. [Robinson](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/) |
| Failure exposure | How often sets reach momentary failure | Do not require failure on every set. A trial found similar quadriceps growth at 1–2 RIR and failure. | Direct but small, short, muscle-specific trial. [Refalo 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/) |
| Load and repetitions | Load, repetitions, effort, technique | Use comfortable loads you can progress. Roughly 5–30 reps covers many practical choices; 6–15 is often convenient. This is a practical band, not a proven pair of biological cutoffs. | Hypertrophy occurs across broad loads with sufficient effort. Heavier loading favors maximal strength more clearly. [Lopez](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/) |
| Frequency and session size | Sessions per muscle and sets within each session | Twice weekly is a convenient way to distribute work. Split sessions when set quality or tolerability deteriorates. | Frequency has little clearly independent hypertrophy effect after volume is considered. No established universal per-session cap. [Pelland](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/) |
| Rest between sets | Whether repetitions and technique recover | Allow about 2–3 minutes for demanding compounds and 1–2 minutes for simpler exercises, longer when needed. | These are practical defaults. A meta-analysis favors longer over very short rests, but did not establish additional hypertrophy benefits beyond roughly 90 seconds. [Singer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11349676/) |
| Exercise selection and range | Target muscle, comfortable range, stability, regional coverage | Use repeatable movements through a comfortable substantial range. Include exercises that challenge the muscle at longer lengths where appropriate. | Promising but muscle-specific. Seated leg curls outperformed prone curls for hamstring volume; a regional meta-analysis found small average differences. [Maeo](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/), [Varovic](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40570881/) |
| Progression | Repetitions or load at comparable RIR, range, and technique | Increase repetitions, then load, when the current task becomes easier. Do not require a personal record every session. | Operational training practice, not a validated growth measurement. More weight achieved by shortening range is not equivalent progress. |
| Protein | Total daily intake relative to body mass | About 1.6 g/kg/day is a useful target; 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day provides a practical buffer for many adults. | Stronger. The often-quoted 1.62 estimate is a population breakpoint with uncertainty, not a precise requirement. [Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/) |
| Energy intake | Weight trend, food intake, waist trend, goal | Avoid a persistent substantial deficit for maximum growth. Maintenance may permit growth; a small surplus is reasonable if needed. | Stronger against large deficits; weaker for an exact optimal surplus. Larger surpluses more clearly increased skinfolds than growth in a small trained-lifter study. [Murphy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/), [Helms](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10620361/) |
| Carbohydrates | Meal adequacy and performance during demanding training | Eat enough to support training and dietary preference after meeting energy and protein needs. | A 2026 meta-analysis found no clear independent hypertrophy advantage from higher carbohydrate intake, with low certainty. Acute performance benefits may depend on fasting and session demands. [Hypertrophy review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41712097/), [Performance review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8878406/) |
| Sleep | Duration, regularity, perceived quality, daytime sleepiness, personal baseline | Provide adequate sleep opportunity, commonly 7–9 hours for adults, and individualize. Address recurring short sleep. | Strong for general recovery importance, limited direct quantification of chronic hypertrophy. Athlete consensus rejects one-size-fits-all precision. [Walsh](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/7/356) |
| Fatigue and stress | Repeated performance decline, persistent fatigue, stress report | Look for several agreeing signals across comparable sessions before changing the plan. | Indirect for hypertrophy. No individual marker diagnoses overtraining or a personal recoverable-volume boundary. [Meeusen](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23247672/) |
| Soreness and pain | Location, persistence, effect on movement | Mild soreness can be ordinary. Reduce or change work that repeatedly compromises the next session; pain changing movement requires a separate assessment. | Soreness and damage are not necessary measures of growth. [Damas](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/) |
| Cardio and other workload | Endurance duration/intensity, sports, physical work, leg fatigue | Keep cardiovascular training. Adjust scheduling and total demands if it repeatedly reduces lifting quality. | Whole-muscle meta-analysis is reassuring; a fiber-level analysis suggests possible small interference. [Schumann](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/), [Lundberg](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35476184/) |
| Creatine | Supplement use and start date | Optional creatine monohydrate, typically 3–5 g/day, for otherwise healthy adults. | Evidence supports a modest adjunct benefit. Direct size estimates are smaller and less certain than some lean-mass claims; water changes complicate measurement. [Burke](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37432300/), [ISSN](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469049/) |
| Consistency and background | Training history, adherence, interruptions, age, health, goals | Adapt the starting workload and expected rate of change to the person. Resume gradually after interruption. | Study averages cannot establish a universal formula across experience, age, health, or sex. The current evidence does not justify demographic “recovery penalties.” |

Do not add fixed hypertrophy thresholds for deep-sleep minutes, HRV, resting heart rate, hydration, micronutrients, menstrual phase, testosterone changes, or “optimal” body-fat percentage. Some can matter in context, but this review did not identify validated mappings from them to an individual's optimal number of sets. This is a map of the main actionable domains, not a claim to enumerate every determinant of muscle biology.

## How to use the evidence in training

The following is a practical feedback process derived from the evidence. Its exact timing and adjustment amounts are **coaching heuristics**, not a tested clinical algorithm.

1. **Establish a repeatable baseline.** Keep exercise selection reasonably stable. Record working sets, repetitions, loads, and estimated RIR. Start from the current sustainable routine, or a modest workload if new or returning. Distribute work across the week to maintain quality.
2. **Make food and sleep adequate.** Aim for the protein target above and sufficient energy. For an 80 kg adult, 1.6–2.2 g/kg is approximately 128–176 g protein daily. Several protein-containing meals are convenient; do not organize the day around a compulsory 30-minute post-workout window. Total intake is the clearer priority. [Protein-timing meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24299050/)
3. **Review performance every few weeks.** Compare the same exercise, range, equipment, and approximate effort. Improvement can justify continuing the current plan. It does not prove a particular amount of muscle growth.
4. **If progress stalls, check the explanation before adding work.** Look at missed sessions, effort estimation, changes in technique, rest times, food intake, sleep, and competing activity. A single poor workout is insufficient evidence of a plateau.
5. **If recovery is good and the target muscle is persistently stagnant, try a small addition.** For example, add one or two weekly sets for that muscle and reassess. Change one major variable at a time. Do not simultaneously add volume, train everything to failure, and cut calories.
6. **If comparable performance repeatedly declines alongside fatigue, try less work.** Remove a few sets or use an easier week while addressing sleep, food, and other demands. A short reduction of roughly 20–30% can be a reasonable experiment, but it is not a researched universal prescription or a wearable-triggered rule.
7. **Evaluate growth on a longer horizon.** Use standardized measurements across roughly 8–12 weeks where practical. Tape measurements and photographs are accessible but noisy; waist, fat, glycogen, water, lighting, and recent exercise can change appearances. MRI or consistently performed ultrasound measures muscle size more directly, but are not necessary for routine self-monitoring.

An isolated bad night should prompt attention to how the session actually feels and performs, not an automatic declaration that training is wasted. Marked sleepiness, illness, or pain can change whether training is appropriate independently of hypertrophy optimization.

Scheduled deloads are an option for fatigue management, not a guaranteed growth enhancer. A trial of one week without resistance training in a nine-week program found no hypertrophy advantage; it does not settle the value of reduced-volume deloads or longer-term fatigue management. [Coleman et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)

## Common optimization claims that need qualification

- **“The best hypertrophy rep range is 8–12.”** A useful convenience, not an exclusive growth zone. Broad loading ranges work. [Load meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/)
- **“Every set must reach failure.”** A failure meta-analysis and a direct RIR trial do not support that requirement. This does not mean very easy work is equally effective. [Refalo review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/), [Refalo trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/)
- **“A big bulk guarantees more muscle.”** The exact surplus is uncertain; faster weight gain can largely add fat. [Helms](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10620361/)
- **“Feeling sore or getting a pump proves growth.”** Neither directly measures durable enlargement. Acute protein-synthesis responses can reflect repair and do not reliably predict individual long-term growth. [Damas](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/), [Mitchell](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24586775/)
- **“The post-workout hormone spike tells us which workout builds more muscle.”** Systemic hormone responses did not determine hypertrophy in a longitudinal trial. [Morton 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27174923/)
- **“More advanced techniques mean more growth.”** Complex equipment, set structures, tempo rules, and periodization did not consistently determine outcomes in the ACSM overview. Use them for preference or practical constraints, not presumed superiority. [ACSM](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/)
- **“Every recovery intervention helps gains.”** Repeated cold-water immersion immediately after lifting may work against hypertrophy. The evidence does not establish a precise universally safe waiting interval. [Cold-water meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11235606/)
- **“Alcohol only matters because it adds calories.”** A small acute study found impaired post-exercise synthesis after a very large alcohol dose, 1.5 g/kg. It supports avoiding binge drinking around training, not calculating the hypertrophy effect of one drink. [Parr](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3922864/)

## Implications for OneRep Progress

This is a research recommendation, not an implemented feature. Existing documentation already distinguishes OneRep wellness estimates from validated measures in [sleep and strain](./sleep-and-strain.md), and preserves illness-related context in [recovery mode](./features/illness-recovery.md). Hypertrophy guidance should retain those boundaries.

**User task:** Understand whether current muscle-building training is productive and choose a sensible next adjustment. Success means the user can explain the evidence behind that adjustment, not maximize a score.

**Proposed content, in priority order:**

1. Muscle-specific training history: completed direct sets, estimated indirect contribution, frequency, and progression on comparable exercises.
2. Outcome history: standardized optional measurements, photographs, and body-weight trends, with clear measurement limitations. Label lifting improvement as performance progress rather than muscle gained.
3. Recovery context: sleep trends, subjective fatigue, soreness affecting training, stress, and active recovery episodes. Keep observations separate from causal claims.
4. Nutrition context: protein coverage and weight direction, using existing logs when available. Missing meals or missing days mean incomplete data, not zero intake.
5. One explainable next step: continue, review technique/effort, try a small volume adjustment, or address a recurring recovery constraint. Show the observation, rationale, uncertainty, and linked evidence.

**Required data discipline:**

- Distinguish planned from completed sets, warm-ups from working sets, and missing RIR from an assumed effort level.
- Explain exercise-to-muscle mappings. A bench-press set is not a universally equal stimulus to chest, triceps, and deltoids.
- Keep time windows visible. A one-night reading and an eight-week training trend are different evidence.
- Compare like with like; changes in equipment, technique, exercise, or range can invalidate a performance comparison.
- Separate data completeness from scientific certainty. More wearable samples do not validate a hypertrophy model.
- Require enough comparable observations before suggesting a trend. Exact minimum data requirements should be tested as product rules, not cited as physiology.
- Preserve “insufficient information.” Do not backfill recovery or food intake with an apparently measured number.
- Annotate illness and return-to-training periods so planned rest is not described as failed adherence.

**Examples, using hypothetical data:**

“Your chest training has stayed at 10 direct sets per week. Bench repetitions at the same load and effort have improved over four weeks. Continuing this amount is reasonable.”

“Your last two comparable leg sessions were below your recent performance, and you reported persistent fatigue. Consider an easier week and review sleep and food intake. These observations do not establish the cause.”

Avoid “hypertrophy readiness 83%,” “you are outside your anabolic zone,” “six hours of sleep reduced your recoverable volume by four sets,” or “you gained 120 g of muscle this week.” No paper identified here validates those inferences.

A future personal target band could represent a workload the user has successfully sustained. Label it as an observed or chosen training range, not a biological optimum. Do not build a narrow moving green zone from an unvalidated weighted sum of sleep, strain, and food.

## What remains unknown

No validated model identified here jointly combines training dose, sleep, stress, protein, energy intake, HRV, and individual background to predict maximum hypertrophy. Interaction effects are particularly under-studied. Most training interventions are short relative to years of lifting, and many focus on a few muscles or young adults. A nonsignificant result can reflect imprecision rather than equivalence.

The useful next product step is to build transparent tracking and modest, testable suggestions around these limits. Any claim that a personal range predicts better growth would require prospective validation against meaningful outcomes and a simpler comparison strategy, not just correlation with an existing recovery score.
