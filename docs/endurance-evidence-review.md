# Endurance training, fueling, and recovery: evidence and actions

Research date: 25 September 2026. Companion to [hypertrophy](./hypertrophy-evidence-review.md) and [deficit/recomp](./deficit-and-recomp-evidence-review.md). Targeted evidence review, not an exhaustive systematic review. Human trials and evidence syntheses take priority. Abstracts and accessible full texts were used; full text was not available for every paper. Scope includes running, cycling, triathlon, and related endurance sports, with sport-specific limitations identified.

## Main finding

Endurance athletes also need an appropriate balance of training and recovery, but no validated universal “optimal zone” can be calculated from workload and a wearable recovery score. Performance depends on aerobic capacity, sustainable intensity, movement economy, durability during prolonged exercise, fueling, and the ability to train consistently.

An athlete can improve performance without a higher VO2max, and can increase VO2max without improving race performance. A lighter body or higher VO2max expressed per kilogram is not automatically better if absolute power, health, or durability declines.

The goal should be **sustainable sport-specific performance**, not maximum training load, minimum body weight, or maximum fat oxidation.

## Evidence that supports and challenges popular prescriptions

| Claim | Supporting evidence | Counterevidence or limit | Useful conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polarized training is superior | A 2024 meta-analysis found a small VO2peak advantage. [Oliveira](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38717713/) | A 2025 individual-participant-data network meta-analysis found no clear overall difference between polarized and pyramidal approaches for VO2max or time-trial performance. [Rosenblat](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39888556/) | Mostly easier work plus purposeful harder training is defensible; exact 80/20 is not a universal optimum. |
| Most endurance training should be easy | Elite observational data commonly show a large low-intensity share, often pyramidal or polarized. [Training-distribution review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37964776/) | What elite athletes do is not a randomized test of what a three-hour-per-week beginner needs. | Match distribution to available time, sport, experience, and event. |
| Strength work helps endurance | Meta-analysis supports improvements in running economy from appropriate strength training. [Llanos-Lagos et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38165636/) | Effects depend on methods and speed; running findings do not establish the best program for swimming or cycling. | Use a manageable strength dose as support for endurance goals. |
| More training is always better | Training provides adaptation stimulus. | Tapering studies show performance can improve after reducing volume while preserving some intensity. [Wang et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37163550/) | Fitness gained and fatigue currently carried are different things. |
| Train low to race faster | Low-carbohydrate sessions can amplify some cellular signals. | A meta-analysis found no clear overall performance benefit from periodized carbohydrate restriction. [Gejl and Nybo](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34001184/) | Mechanistic signals do not justify making underfueling the default. |
| Burning more fat means better endurance | Ketogenic diets increase fat oxidation. | Elite race-walker studies found impaired economy and performance benefit despite greater fat oxidation. [Burke 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28012184/), [Replication 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32497061/) | Fuel choice should support performance, not a fat-burning metric. |
| Higher carbohydrate intake is always better | Some trained athletes oxidize more ingested carbohydrate at 120 versus 90 g/hour. [Podlogar et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35951130/) | Oxidation is not equivalent to faster races, and tolerance differs. A 2026 review proposes individualization. [Contemporary review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41759826/) | Higher intakes are an advanced option to practice, not a universal minimum. |
| HRV identifies the optimal workout | HRV-guided training is a plausible way to individualize load. | Meta-analysis found small favorable estimates without clear superiority in fitness/performance. [Manresa-Rocamora et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34639599/) | HRV is one contextual input, not a command or direct measure of muscle recovery. |
| The weekly 10% rule prevents injury | Avoiding abrupt load changes is reasonable. | Reviews show inconsistent associations. A 2025 cohort linked injury with single-session distance increases relative to the previous 30-day maximum, not proof of a preventive weekly rule. [Review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34478518/), [Cohort](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12421110/) | Inspect individual sessions and recent history; do not declare a sharp safe/unsafe cutoff. |

## Training criteria and practical actions

The suggestions below are adaptable starting practices. Exact weekly numbers are coaching heuristics unless attributed to a specific experimental finding.

| Criterion | Track | Practical action | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sport and event | Sport, distance, terrain, duration, race date | Match key sessions to event demands | Marathon running, track cycling, and ultra-trail require different preparation |
| Training history | Consistent recent weeks, interruptions, injury history | Build from sustained recent capacity | A previous lifetime best is not current readiness |
| Volume | Time, distance, elevation, and sport-specific mechanical demands | Progress gradually and avoid changing everything simultaneously | Equal training hours do not mean equal tissue load across sports |
| Intensity | Pace/power, perceived effort, heart rate, threshold definitions | Keep easy work easy enough to support consistency; add purposeful quality work | Heat, fatigue, terrain, and sensor error change heart rate and pace |
| Hard-session frequency | Intervals, threshold sessions, races, demanding long sessions | For many recreational athletes, one or two quality sessions weekly is a reasonable starting point | Not a universal evidence-derived optimum; a long session can itself be demanding |
| Distribution | Time in zones and session purpose | Use predominantly lower-intensity work as volume grows | 80% of sessions is not the same as 80% of minutes |
| Long-session demand | Duration/distance versus recent longest sessions; fueling | Build the long session deliberately rather than hiding a large jump in an acceptable weekly total | Cohort associations cannot predict an individual's injury probability |
| Strength training | Consistency, movement quality, soreness interfering with sport | Often begin with two manageable sessions weekly, adjusting in competition periods | Heavy lifting and plyometrics require preparation; maximum hypertrophy volume is not the goal |
| Recovery | Sleep, fatigue, mood, muscle/joint symptoms, comparable session performance | Interpret several signals together; adjust when decline persists | One low HRV reading is not a diagnosis |
| Taper | Race priority, accumulated fatigue, sport | A reduced-volume period retaining some intensity can improve performance | Meta-analysis supports roughly 41–60% volume reduction and up to three weeks, not an identical taper for everyone |
| Progress | Repeated sport-specific time trials, submaximal pace/power at comparable effort, event outcomes | Compare conditions and allow familiarization | VO2max alone misses economy, durability, and execution |

**Zone definitions must be explicit.** In a common three-zone research model, zone 1 is below the first lactate/ventilatory threshold, zone 2 lies between thresholds, and zone 3 is above the second. This “zone 2” is not the same as the easy zone 2 of many five-zone watches. Pyramidal means most time low, less moderate, least high; polarized means most low, more high than moderate. Threshold testing or a carefully interpreted talk test can help orient effort, but generic percentages of age-predicted maximum heart rate do not locate everyone's thresholds precisely.

**A reasonable adjustment loop:** Build a repeatable block, observe both completion and performance, then alter one major variable. If easy sessions become repeatedly harder at comparable pace/power, review heat, illness, fueling, sleep, and total workload before concluding that fitness declined. If improving and tolerating the work, continue. If several indicators deteriorate, reduce demands and investigate the cause rather than chase a load target. This loop is an evidence-informed practice, not a validated numerical algorithm.

## Fueling: what to act on

Carbohydrate and total energy are related but distinct. Adequate calories from fat and protein do not always provide adequate carbohydrate for demanding training. Conversely, a good gel strategy during one workout does not ensure adequate daily energy intake.

| Situation | Reasonable starting guidance | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Daily carbohydrate | Roughly 3–5 g/kg on light days, 5–7 with moderate training, 6–10 with higher endurance loads | Broad sports-nutrition reference ranges, not mandatory targets by app activity category |
| Before demanding exercise | About 1–4 g/kg in the 1–4 hours beforehand, adapted to timing, appetite, and gastrointestinal tolerance | Not every short easy session needs a large pre-workout meal |
| During about 1–2.5 hours of demanding exercise | Approximately 30–60 g carbohydrate/hour | Needs depend on intensity, duration, prior food, and tolerance |
| During prolonged events beyond roughly 2.5–3 hours | Up to about 90 g/hour using suitable carbohydrate mixtures | Practice progressively; higher intake is not automatically better |
| Advanced high-demand endurance fueling | Some well-practiced athletes may consider 90–120 g/hour | Emerging evidence; not a default for beginners or every sport |
| Short turnaround between hard sessions | Rapid carbohydrate replacement matters more when the next session is within hours | Common guidance is about 1.0–1.2 g/kg/hour in early recovery; less urgency with a full recovery day |
| Protein | Often approximately 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day as a practical starting range | Total workload, energy restriction, and combined strength goals can change needs |

The conventional ranges come from [Thomas, Erdman and Burke, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26891166/) and [Burke et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21660838/). Higher-intake guidance is discussed in the [2026 carbohydrate review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41759826/). These are starting ranges, not a set of requirements to sum regardless of energy needs. Food and drink consumed during exercise count toward daily intake.

For example, 60 g/hour during a two-hour ride is 120 g carbohydrate, approximately 480 kcal. In a deficit-oriented app, that should appear as planned fueling rather than a failure to restrict. A sports drink, gel, or ordinary food can contribute; choice depends on practical access and tolerance.

**Train the fueling plan.** A systematic review supports investigating repeated feeding practice to improve tolerance, but protocols and outcomes vary. Introduce changes during training, record intake and gastrointestinal symptoms, and avoid first testing a high intake on race day. [Gut-training review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37061651/)

**Hydration:** Individualize to conditions, sweat losses, duration, and opportunities to drink. Thirst is useful, especially for preventing forced overdrinking; demanding heat conditions may warrant a practiced plan. Avoid drinking so much that body mass increases during prolonged exercise. Sodium-containing drinks do not make excessive fluid intake harmless. There is no universal liters/hour or sodium/hour prescription suitable for every athlete. [Exercise-associated hyponatremia consensus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26102445/)

**Iron:** Unexplained fatigue and underperformance can warrant assessment, particularly where iron deficiency risk is elevated. Do not infer iron deficiency from heart rate or recommend iron routinely without evaluation. Evidence for supplementation depends on baseline status, and better blood iron markers do not guarantee better performance. [Oral-iron meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38407751/)

## Deficits, energy availability, and endurance

Energy balance compares intake with total expenditure. Energy availability is a research construct estimating energy remaining after exercise, relative to fat-free mass:

`EA = (energy intake - exercise energy expenditure) / fat-free mass`

Units are kcal/kg fat-free mass/day. Exercise expenditure is not total daily expenditure. Published calculations depend on methodological choices, including how exercise expenditure is defined. Food logs, wearables, and body-composition estimates introduce substantial uncertainty.

The often-quoted **30 kcal/kg fat-free mass/day** is not a universal diagnostic threshold, and 45 is not a guaranteed-safe score. Responses vary across people, sexes, body systems, severity, and exposure duration. A numerical crossing cannot diagnose or exclude REDs. Weight stability can coexist with underfueling. [IOC REDs consensus](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994)

Controlled evidence makes the concern more than theoretical: a small randomized crossover study in female endurance athletes found impaired exercise performance after two weeks of low energy availability. This is important causal evidence but not a personal cutoff or a prediction for every male or female athlete. [Caldwell et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38936255/)

**Practical phase choices, based on synthesis rather than direct head-to-head trials:**

- During a demanding build or race preparation, prioritize adequate fueling and performance. Do not combine escalating workload with aggressive weight loss by default.
- If deliberate fat loss is appropriate, a lower-demand period usually offers more flexibility. Use a modest, monitored deficit and preserve key-session fueling.
- An acceptable weekly calorie average does not establish that individual long or intense sessions were adequately fueled.
- After illness, injury, or a period of problematic underfueling, restore training and nutrition deliberately. Do not use stable scale weight as clearance to resume a deficit.
- Menstrual changes, recurrent bone-stress injuries, persistent fatigue, reduced libido, and unexplained performance decline deserve assessment, not an automatic calorie cut or an app diagnosis.

## Endurance with strength or hypertrophy goals

Concurrent endurance and resistance training generally can improve both capacities. A whole-muscle meta-analysis found no clear average interference with hypertrophy or maximal strength, while explosive strength was more vulnerable. A separate fiber-level analysis suggested possible small interference. Neither result means unlimited combined workloads are easy to recover from. [Schumann](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/), [Lundberg](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35476184/)

Choose the phase priority. An endurance-focused runner may need enough lifting for economy and strength, not a bodybuilder's maximum recoverable leg volume. A hypertrophy-focused person can retain cardio while managing its demands. Where practical, separate demanding lower-body lifting from key endurance work, or put the priority session first. Exact optimal spacing is not established for every combination, and calendar constraints matter.

## What OneRep Progress could usefully show

Research recommendation only, without implementation:

1. **Sport-specific progress:** recent training consistency, event-specific tests, comparable pace/power and effort, and race preparation stage. Include absolute power alongside weight-normalized metrics where relevant.
2. **Training distribution:** duration and purpose of sessions, with explicit zone definitions. Separate sport types rather than merging all hours into one equivalent load.
3. **Demand changes:** unusual long sessions and shifts in intensity or frequency, described as changes to review rather than injury predictions.
4. **Fueling practice:** planned and logged carbohydrate per hour for long/key sessions, meal opportunities, tolerance, and recovery turnaround.
5. **Recovery context:** subjective fatigue, sleep, symptoms, and optional HRV trends, with data completeness and device provenance.
6. **One explainable next step:** continue the block, practice fueling, adjust a demanding session, review persistent fatigue, or prepare a taper.

Example using hypothetical data: “Your long rides are now over two hours, but no during-ride fueling is recorded. If you have been eating during them, add it; otherwise, consider practicing a carbohydrate plan.” Missing logs should not become a claim that the athlete fasted.

Avoid “your safe training ceiling is 72,” “you are 91% ready,” “your HRV proves overtraining,” or “lose another kilogram to improve your VO2max.” This review identifies no validation for those personalized claims.

## Remaining uncertainty

Evidence is strongest for broad principles: train consistently, make intensity purposeful, fuel demanding work, retain appropriate strength training, and adapt to persistent problems. It is much weaker for exact personal load ceilings, daily recovery-based prescriptions, or a universal best intensity distribution. Research in women, masters athletes, ultra-endurance events, and long-term mixed-goal programs remains uneven. Track useful observations and state uncertainty rather than turning those gaps into precise scores.
